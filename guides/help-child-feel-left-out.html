How to Help a Child Who Feels Left Out

Gentle conversation starters and practical friendship skills for children who feel excluded from play or group activities.

A practical guide for parents and caregivers

1. Listen before solving

Ask what happened and how your child felt. Resist immediately naming another child as the problem; there may be more to the story.

2. Name the feeling

Try: “It sounds lonely when you want to join and the game has already started.” Children often need language for their feelings before they can plan their next step.

3. Practice an easy invitation

Role-play a simple opener: “Can I play too?” or “Would you like to build with me?” Keep practice light and playful, not a performance test.

4. Offer more than one path

If a group says no, help your child think of another activity, another friend, or a way to invite someone into their own game. Respect other children’s boundaries too.

5. Check in with trusted adults

If exclusion repeats or your child seems distressed, talk with a teacher or caregiver about patterns and supportive opportunities to connect.

A five-minute friendship practice

With toys or puppets, act out joining a game. Practice a friendly question, listening to the answer, and suggesting another activity if the group is already full. Swap roles so your child can try both sides of the conversation.

Words to use in the moment

Try “That sounds disappointing. Do you want to tell me what happened?” After listening, ask “Would you like to think of something to say next time, or do you just need a hug?”

Frequently asked question

Should I make another child include mine?

Encourage kindness and inclusive opportunities, but avoid forcing friendship. Adults can support safe group play and address repeated exclusion or bullying.

Every child is different. These general ideas are not a substitute for personalized professional support.
