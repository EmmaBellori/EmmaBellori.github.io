How to Teach Kids Good Sportsmanship

Good sportsmanship is not simply “being happy when you lose.” It is learning how to compete, feel disappointed, follow the rules and still treat other people with respect.

A practical guide for parents and caregivers

Teach the routine before the game starts

Children do better when expectations are concrete. Before playing, agree on a few simple rules: take turns, follow the same rules for everyone, keep hands and pieces safe, and finish with one respectful sentence. A predictable routine is easier to remember than a lecture after emotions are already high.

Model what you want to hear

Children notice how adults react to mistakes, unfair calls and losses. Let them hear you say things such as “Nice move,” “I thought I had that one,” or “Good game.” When you lose, show that disappointment can exist alongside respect.

Praise behavior, not just victory

Instead of focusing only on who won, notice choices that make play enjoyable: waiting for a turn, trying again, following a rule even when it is inconvenient, congratulating someone else, or recovering after frustration. Specific feedback tells a child exactly what they did well.

Make room for disappointment

A child can be a good sport and still feel upset. Avoid requiring a cheerful smile immediately after a loss. A more realistic goal is respectful behavior while the feeling passes. “You can be disappointed and still say thanks for playing” is clearer than “Don’t be a sore loser.”

Teach one recovery phrase

Give children a sentence they can use when they do not know what to say: “Good game,” “Can we play again later?” or “I need a break.” Rehearse the phrase away from the game so it is easier to reach for when emotions are strong.

A simple family sportsmanship rule

At the end of every game, each player says one positive thing about the play rather than the result: a clever move, a funny moment, a good effort or something they learned.

Let children see both sides

When the moment is calm, ask how the other player may have felt. Perspective-taking helps children understand why gloating, blaming or changing the rules can make games less fun for everyone. Keep the conversation curious rather than accusatory.

Use short games for practice

If losing is very difficult, practice with games that end quickly. Several short, manageable experiences give a child more chances to rehearse the ending routine than one long game that becomes emotionally exhausting.

What if sportsmanship disappears as soon as my child loses?

Return to the smallest skill. Instead of asking for perfect behavior, work on one step at a time: pause, keep objects safe, use the agreed phrase, or walk away respectfully. Improvement can be gradual.

Every child is different. These general ideas are educational and are not a substitute for individualized professional advice.
